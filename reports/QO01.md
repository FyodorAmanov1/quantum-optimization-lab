# QO01: Feasible uniform-sampling baseline

## Question

How does the existing p=1 penalty-based QAOA distribution compare with uniform sampling over all bitstrings and uniform sampling over feasible portfolios, when feasibility, conditional objective, and the exact optimality gap are reported separately?

## Method and assumptions

The unchanged synthetic instance uses NumPy seed 1729, six assets, and exactly three selected assets. Returns are drawn uniformly from [0.01, 0.08); the covariance is `factor @ factor.T / (100*n)` for a standard-normal square factor. These are artificial inputs, with no market data or calibrated time horizon. Holdings are binary units, not normalized investment weights.

Minimize `f(x) = 2*x.T@covariance@x - returns@x`, with penalty energy `f(x) + (sum(x)-3)**2`. The risk coefficient is 2 and the penalty coefficient is 1. No transaction costs, fees, spreads, market impact, or hardware/monetary costs are modeled. The mean-variance motivation comes from [Markowitz (1952)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1952.tb01525.x), while this binary toy formulation has narrower scope.

All 64 strings and their objectives are enumerated. Exact minimization over the 20 feasible strings gives the reference optimum. The existing QAOA starts in the uniform superposition, applies a cost phase and transverse-X mixing, and selects the minimum expected penalized energy among 17 gamma values in [0, 2*pi] and 17 beta values in [0, pi]. This uses 289 exact CPU statevector evaluations with 64 amplitudes each. The QAOA family is described by [Farhi, Goldstone and Gutmann (2014)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1411.4028); this experiment does not reproduce their graph-specific performance guarantees.

The two new baseline laws are `p_all(x)=1/64` and `p_feasible(x)=1/20` on feasible strings (zero elsewhere). A common evaluator computes exact expectations under all three sampling laws. It does not draw finite samples, estimate confidence intervals, or report a best-of-many statistic. Enumerating the laws removes sampling noise on this small instance.

For each law p and feasible set F, the metrics are:

- Feasibility: `q = sum(p[x] for x in F)` before rejection.
- Conditional objective: `sum(p[x]*f(x) for x in F)/q`.
- Additive optimality gap: conditional objective minus the exact feasible minimum; lower is better.
- Expected independent draws per accepted portfolio: `1/q`, excluding setup and QAOA training.

Undefined conditional quantities at zero feasible mass are emitted as JSON null, with input validation and a floating-point bound on feasibility. Additive gaps remain meaningful for the negative optimum here. Both uniform laws have the same conditional distribution: conditioning the all-string law on F cancels its constant probability. Its feasibility is `C(6,3)/2**6 = 20/64`; the feasible-only law has feasibility one.

The implementation constructs both baseline vectors using the shared enumeration. For this particular constraint an operational classical sampler can choose three distinct indices uniformly, without enumerating strings or rejecting samples. That sampler's runtime is not benchmarked. Neither baseline requires parameter tuning. Enumeration, optimizer training, distribution construction, and per-draw rejection costs must not be conflated.

## Actual findings

The regenerated [results/latest.json](../results/latest.json), using NumPy 2.2.4, records the following rounded values. The exact feasible optimum is **-0.150447457870**, at `[0,1,1,1,0,0]`.

| Distribution | Feasibility | Conditional objective | Additive optimality gap | Draws per feasible portfolio |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| QAOA p=1 | 0.647345892559 | -0.095477761671 | 0.054969696199 | 1.544769205296 |
| Uniform all bitstrings | 0.312500000000 | -0.095220318098 | 0.055227139771 | 3.200000000000 |
| Uniform feasible portfolios | 1.000000000000 | -0.095220318098 | 0.055227139771 | 1.000000000000 |

The selected QAOA angles remain gamma=0.392699081699 and beta=2.945243112740. Its expected penalized objective is 0.336844677219, versus 1.402011968300 for uniform bitstrings and -0.095220318098 for uniform feasible portfolios. These penalized values include infeasible outcomes and are distinct from the conditional objectives.

QAOA raises feasibility by about 0.334846 compared with uniform bitstrings. Its conditional objective is only about 0.000257444 lower than either uniform law, and its additive gap remains about 0.054970. The stronger feasible-only baseline always meets cardinality without parameter tuning, while QAOA still rejects about 35.27% of its draws. This negative finding is retained: improvement over unrestricted random strings does not establish a compelling result against feasible classical sampling. The experiment does not show that any method reaches the optimum in its average feasible sample.

## Limitations and interview understanding

This is one synthetic instance and one coarse p=1 grid, optimized for penalized expectation rather than conditional quality. It is an exponential CPU simulation with exact training expectations, no hardware noise, and no finite-shot training or evaluation. It supports no novelty, general performance, practical investment, speedup, or quantum advantage claim. Expected rejection draws assume independent repeated sampling and do not include training or setup. Feasible sampling can be difficult for more general constraints; the fixed-cardinality baseline here is particularly simple.

The author should be able to derive `C(n,k)/2**n`, show why the two uniform laws have identical conditional objectives, and explain why feasibility must be reported before postselection. They should distinguish penalized expectation, feasible conditional objective, and the exact optimum, and explain why a negative objective makes naive objective ratios misleading. They should also explain the cost-phase/mixer steps and why the penalty and transverse-X mixer do not enforce cardinality exactly. Feasibility-preserving mixers are discussed by [Hadfield et al. (2019)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.03489); none is implemented here. Finally, they should explain why 289 ideal statevector evaluations and a small improvement on one instance cannot establish computational advantage.
